Fifteen words. The entire product.

**The one moment.** Open the bag. Find the number. Look it up. Meet the kid. Every other surface exists to deliver that moment, or to convert what happens after.

**Four pieces of copy ship. Nothing else at launch.**

1. **Homepage hero.**
   > One shirt. One kid. Open the bag to meet them.

2. **The tagline.** Wherever there's room for one line: under the hero, on /shirts, in the Stripe checkout description, on /shirts/success, in the drip email, above the reveal-page CTA.
   > The shirt is how you meet them. $25 a month is how you stay.

   >

   >

   > **[ Stay with [Name] — $25/month ]**

   (Render "their/them" in the static template, or template-substitute the kid's actual pronoun. Don't ship "him/his" hard-coded — half the canonical roster is girls.)

   > Subject: *Your kid is waiting.*
   >

## 1. What the research says

Seven findings, none of which are mine. All linked at the bottom.

**The curiosity gap is built from partial information, not no information.** George Loewenstein's information-gap theory says curiosity spikes when a person discovers a *specific* hole in what they know. Complete ignorance produces nothing — a buyer who has no idea what BAN is feels no pull toward a shirt. A buyer who knows there's a school in Omoro with a roster of kids, that each kid has a number, and that one of those numbered kids is about to be theirs — but doesn't know which one — feels a pull. The priming dose has to land first; mystery only works downstream of clarity about what the mystery is.

**Anticipation is the dopamine spike, not delivery.** Mystery-box and reward-pathway research is consistent: the biggest hit of pleasurable arousal comes *before* the box opens. The wait is the product. Variable-ratio reinforcement (the slot-machine pattern — you know a reward is coming but not when or what) is the strongest motivator behavioral psychology has documented. Translated to BAN: the days between buying the shirt and the shirt arriving in the mail are not dead time. That window is the most emotionally valuable real estate in the entire funnel.

**Specific named victims convert. Statistics don't.** Paul Slovic and colleagues (Kogut, Ritov, Small) have shown across multiple studies that people give significantly more to one specific, named, faced child than to an identical-but-statistical group of children. Baby Jessica raised $700K. Five hundred thousand starving kids raise less. The mechanism is "spontaneous affective reaction" — a single identifiable person triggers emotional response that statistics flatten out. The implication for BAN is sharp: the moment a statistic ("kids in Omoro") becomes a specific person ("this is Aaron, he likes football, his English teacher is Tonny") is the moment a buyer converts from t-shirt customer to monthly sponsor. The reveal IS the conversion moment.

**The unboxing ritual is the social-share trigger.** Roughly 95% of purchase decisions are emotional. Sixty-something percent of consumers watch unboxing videos when researching a product. Products with substantial unboxing content see sales lifts in the double digits. The shirt-arrival moment for a BAN buyer is functionally an unboxing — and the kid reveal *inside* that unboxing is the content. Designed well, it becomes the most shareable moment in the entire customer journey.

**Memory is dominated by the peak and the end (Kahneman).** Whatever the most intense moment of an experience was, plus how it ended, is what the person remembers and decides from. A long pleasant flat experience is remembered as flat. A short experience with a sharp emotional peak and a satisfying close is remembered as great. For BAN: the peak is the reveal-meeting moment. The end (in the sense of the experience closing into ongoing relationship) is the first letter/photo/quote from the named kid. Both have to be designed for memory, not just function.

**Choice is friction. No-choice is commitment.** Barry Schwartz's paradox-of-choice work shows that more options reduce satisfaction and increase regret. Once a commitment closes a door, the buyer's sense of investment goes up. The fact that BAN buyers don't pick their kid — the shirt picks the kid — is not a limitation we apologize for. It is a feature that eliminates the comparison loop ("should I have picked a different kid?") and forces real commitment to whoever shows up.

**Real scarcity converts. Fake scarcity destroys trust.** Limited-time and limited-supply tactics raise conversion (Nielsen has measured 200%+ lifts in the right conditions). But fake scarcity — countdown timers that reset, "only 3 left" that never decreases — measurably damages brand trust, sometimes 40%+. BAN has *real* scarcity: a real roster of specifically named children, real numbered shirts that map to them, and a cycle mechanic that's honest, not manufactured. The roster size changes as kids age out or new kids join — that's not a marketing number, it's an internal one, and copy should never quote it. We can lean into the scarcity without ever putting a count on it.

## 2. What this means for BAN, specifically

The mystery has to be tight. Mystery about which specific kid is on the other side of the package the buyer's going to open. Transparency about everything else.

The actual fulfillment mechanic matters here, and I had it wrong in the first draft. Kevin doesn't assign numbers to buyers. Shirts get printed with numbers ahead of time and stocked on a shelf by size and color. When an order comes in, whichever Small Black Tee (or whatever) is next in the pile gets packed and shipped. Kevin doesn't know which number went to which buyer. Nobody knows until the buyer opens the box, finds the number on the shirt, and searches it at beanumber.org. **The shelf decides. Not the buyer, not BAN.**

That mechanic is the brand. Don't paper over it. The honest version of the trade is: **you buy a shirt. You don't pick a kid — and we don't either. Whichever shirt happens to be next in the pile is the one that ships. When it arrives, you find the number on it, look it up, and that's the moment you meet your kid.**

The funnel breaks into four psychological stages, each with a different copy job:

1. **Pre-purchase (homepage, /shirts, ads).** Job: establish the priming dose. Tell the buyer that there's a school with a roster of kids, each with a number, and that buying a shirt is how a buyer ends up with one of those kids. Do not lean on the country name or the district — most American buyers don't know where Uganda is and "Omoro" means nothing to them at this stage. The geographic specificity belongs on the partner-transparency pages, after a buyer has decided to care. *Do not* show photos of every kid on the homepage. *Do* show shirts. The kid faces are the payoff, not the bait.

2. **Purchase moment (Stripe checkout, /shirts/success).** Job: lock in the commitment psychologically. They've already swiped. Now the copy has to make the wait feel earned, not boring. "Your shirt is on its way. We don't know what number is on it. You'll find out when you open it." The variable-ratio language belongs here — not "shipping in 5–7 business days" (functional) but "the next time you check the mail could be the day you meet your kid" (anticipation).

3. **The wait (drip emails, days 0–7).** Job: hold the curiosity gap open without breaking it. Day 0: confirmation, set the stakes, name the mechanic explicitly. Day 3: zoom in on the campus without revealing any kid (a teacher's quote, a meal description, the chapel, the football pitch). Day 5–7: imminence — "your shirt is on its way" — without naming a number, because we don't have one. The wait is the product.

4. **Reveal + relationship (shirt arrives, /[N] page).** Job: deliver the peak and start the relationship. The buyer opens the box, finds the number, types it into beanumber.org/[N], and lands on the kid page. That page is the unboxing payload. The IVE (identifiable victim effect) fires here — an abstract "kid far away" becomes "this is Aaron, here's his face, here's his quote, here's the village he walks home to." The country name lands here too, by the way — once a buyer has a specific face and name and teacher in front of them, "Northern Uganda" stops being a foreign abstraction and becomes "where Aaron lives." This is the only place geography pulls its weight. The $25/month ask sits on this page; it converts on this page, because this page is the peak of the experience.

The strategic insight underneath all this: **mystery is the wrapper, identifiable specificity is the payload.** We hide the kid until the buyer has committed *and* opened the box. Then we hand them a real, named, photographed, quoted, specific child — not a category. The mystery does the conversion work for the t-shirt. The identifiable specificity does the conversion work for the monthly sponsorship.

## 3. Framework rules (the guardrails)

Four rules that govern every piece of copy we write under this strategy. If a candidate violates one, it's out.

**Rule 1: Mystery only about the match. Never about the cause.** What the buyer doesn't know yet: which specific kid is on the other side of the shirt they're going to open. What the buyer always knows: BAN is a 501(c)(3) supporting specifically named kids at a specifically named school in Omoro District, Northern Uganda; the YDO team runs the campus; $25/month covers school fees, meals, medical care, mentorship; the shirt is $X and the sponsorship is $25/month and the two are separate trades. We are never mysterious about who we are, who runs the campus, where the money goes, or what the buyer's signing up for financially.

**Rule 2: The reveal has to be worth the wait.** If we promise anticipation, the payoff has to deliver. That means the /[N] kid page on arrival day has to land — a real face, a real quote in the kid's voice, the family context, the teacher's name. If a kid's intake fields are sparse, the reveal feels like a letdown. This rule has a downstream engineering implication: Simon's intake quality is now part of the conversion funnel, not just a content backlog. We can't ship the mystery copy until the roster pages reliably have the goods.

**Rule 3: Voice rules win every tie.** No "generous," no "impact" as a verb, no "empowerment," no "just $25." Faith-rooted not faith-forward. Community-led not savior-driven. Direct over diplomatic. The mystery angle does not become a license to write more cinematic. If anything, the language gets terser — mystery whispered is more powerful than mystery announced.

**Rule 4: We don't know either. Don't fake that we do.** No copy implies BAN is "matching" or "assigning" the buyer to a specific kid. We aren't. The shirts get printed with numbers and stocked on the shelf; whichever shirt is next in the pile of the buyer's size/color is the one that ships; neither Kevin nor the BAN system knows which number went out until the buyer looks it up. Lean into that. "We don't know either" is more honest and more shareable than "we picked the perfect kid for you." Two related implications: (a) we cannot reference a specific number in any pre-arrival communication, because we don't have one to reference; (b) we cannot send a number-keyed link in shipping or delivery notifications, because we don't have the number. The buyer surfaces the number by opening the box and typing it in.

## 3.5. Conversion psychology — the levers the mystery alone won't pull

The mystery framework is the front half of the funnel — it gets the t-shirt sold. The back half is the conversion that actually matters for the business model: shirt-buyer → $25/month sponsor at the moment of reveal. Industry benchmark for one-time-donor → monthly conversion is around 6%. BAN's reveal page is a designed peak moment with maximum identifiable-victim activation; played right, it should significantly outperform that benchmark. Played poorly, it'll undershoot it.

Seven specific psychological levers from established research apply here, most of them concentrated on Surface 10 (the reveal page).

**Lever 1: Endowed progress (Nunes & Drèze, 2006).** People complete tasks faster when they feel they've already started them. A car-wash loyalty card pre-filled with 2 of 10 stamps was redeemed at 34%; an identical-effort card requiring 8 stamps from scratch was redeemed at 19%. Same actual work, near-double completion. Implication for BAN: the shirt purchase IS step 1 of a relationship, not the end of a transaction. The reveal page should frame the $25/month as the next step in something already started, not a new ask. A visible progress marker — *Step 1: Bought the shirt ✓ &nbsp; Step 2: Met [FirstName] ✓ &nbsp; Step 3: Stay in their life* — activates this directly and costs nothing to ship.

**Lever 2: Identity > price (Cialdini commitment & consistency).** Monthly-giving conversion research consistently finds that copy framed around identity ("be the one in Aaron's life") outperforms copy framed around dollar amount ("$25/month covers..."). Once installed, identity carries status-quo bias, habit, and consistency forward — which is precisely what protects the recurring subscription from churn. The first draft of the reveal-page CTA led with money; that's a price-anchored frame. The rewritten version leads with identity, then surfaces price as the implementation detail.

**Lever 3: The visceral dollar breakdown.** "$25/month" is abstract. The buyer's brain has no reference point for it except other $25-shaped things they spend money on (Netflix, a meal, whatever). A specific breakdown — *$11 school fees. $9 for posho and beans every day at lunch. $5 for the campus clinic when malaria hits.* — does two jobs: (a) makes $25 feel calculated rather than arbitrary, (b) gives the buyer a sensory image of what their money is buying. This is the brand's voice rule on specificity, applied to pricing.

**Lever 4: Active pre-commitment at checkout (Cialdini).** Cialdini's research is unambiguous: commitments that are active, freely chosen, public, and effortful stick harder than passive or silent ones. A checkout-page checkbox — *"I'll take a photo when I open it and send it to BAN [optional]"* — does three things at once: increases follow-through on the unboxing moment (variable-ratio reinforcement), generates UGC content for social, and pre-installs the buyer's identity as someone who shares the moment. Cheap, optional, and high-leverage.

**Lever 5: Real, honest, time-bounded scarcity.** Aaron is one specific kid. Only one person sponsors Aaron at a time. If a buyer lands on /[N] and doesn't sponsor, the "spot" can honestly hold for some window (a week feels right) before becoming available to the next visitor. This is the inverse of fake-scarcity timers — Nielsen and others have measured 200%+ conversion lifts from honest scarcity, but ~45% trust damage from fake scarcity. The mechanic has to be real: if we say the spot holds for 7 days, we have to actually hold it and actually release it. If we can commit to that engineering, the copy lever is one of the strongest in the funnel.

**Lever 6: Reciprocity through information already given (Cialdini).** The reveal page hands the buyer everything we know about Aaron — name, face, story, teacher, village, daily life — before asking for anything. Reciprocity is the principle that says people feel pulled to give back after receiving. The CTA copy should subtly acknowledge that trade. Not "consider sponsoring" (passive) and not "we need your help" (institutional) but something like *"You know him now. Stay in his life."* — which both names the gift just given and asks for the response.

**Lever 7: The second mystery.** The first mystery resolves at the reveal: *who's my kid.* But the moment that resolves, a new open loop opens: *what happens to Aaron next?* Does he pass his exams? Does his village get the water project? Does his English keep getting better? Does he end up teaching in his own classroom one day? The sponsorship is how you stay in that second mystery. This is the strongest long-term retention lever I can find, and the first draft of the doc didn't use it at all. The reveal-page CTA should frame sponsorship not as ongoing charity but as *the way you find out what happens next.*

These seven levers stack. Each one is a small lift on its own; deployed together on the reveal page, they should compound. The Surface 10 rewrite below applies all of them.

## 4. Candidate matrix — 13 surfaces

For each surface I wrote three candidates from three different angles. Not "v1 v2 v3 same idea" — three genuinely different psychological hooks:

- **Anticipation:** lean on the wait, the not-yet-knowing, the dopamine of the upcoming reveal. ("You won't know who until it arrives.")
- **Inversion:** lean on the no-choice, shirt-picks-kid mechanic — flipping the usual donor "choose your sponsored child" model. ("You don't pick. The shirt picks.")
- **Promise:** lean on the eventual identifiable-victim payoff — the specific real kid waiting on the other side. ("Every number has a name. Yours is on the way.")

My recommended pick per surface is bolded. Where I'd combine two, I say so.

### Surface 1 — Homepage hero (above the fold)

The cold buyer's first second on the site. Loewenstein's information-gap research says a person needs *partial* information to feel curiosity — complete ignorance produces nothing. So the headline has to deliver the priming dose ("shirts = kids") *and* the mystery hook ("you don't know which") in the same beat. The first draft only delivered the mystery, which meant a cold visitor had to read the sub-headline to figure out what the mystery was even about. Lost attention.

| Angle | Copy |
| --- | --- |
| Anticipation | **One shirt. One kid. You won't know which until you open the box.** |
| Inversion | Every shirt is a different kid. You don't pick. We don't either. |
| Promise | The shirt is how you meet them. $25 a month is how you stay. |

**Pick: Anticipation.** Three concrete nouns and the entire mechanic in 12 words. "Kid" arrives in the second sentence (not the second paragraph), which gives the priming dose; "you won't know which" gives the gap. The Promise angle is the brand's two-trade line from the shirt insert — strong, but it's a tagline, not a headline; it belongs lower on the page or on the reveal CTA itself.

Sub-headline under it: *Every shirt has a real kid behind it — name, village, favorite subject, the whole thing. The number on the shirt you order has already been picked, but neither of us will know which one it is until the package lands at your door. Open it, look the number up, and meet them.*

### Surface 10 — /[N] reveal page (the kid page on arrival day)

This is the peak. The entire funnel exists to deliver the buyer here in the right emotional state. Everything else in this doc is upstream of this page. The first draft underwrote it; the rewrite below applies all seven conversion levers from Section 3.5.

The page breaks into five moments, in order. Each has a copy job. None of them can be skipped or compressed.

**Moment A — Pre-reveal (the hold-button gate)**

The hold-button itself is a ritual: physical effort that converts the reveal into a peak moment in Kahneman's terms. The copy on the gate should *name the wait* (acknowledging the days that have passed since purchase) and *acknowledge the kid was always there.*

| Angle | Copy |
| --- | --- |
| Anticipation | **You've waited. They've been here the whole time. Hold to meet them.** |
| Inversion | This is the kid your shirt belongs to. Hold to see who. |
| Promise | A real name. A real face. Hold to meet them. |

**Pick: Anticipation.**

**Moment B — The reveal itself (first second after the hold releases)**

The kid's name in large serif type. Their photo, full-bleed, large. Then one line: *This is [FirstName]. The shirt you opened is theirs.*

That's it. Nothing else competes for attention in the first second. The IVE (identifiable victim effect) needs an unbroken moment to fire — a single specific human, named and faced, with no clutter around them. Resist the urge to add anything else here.

**Moment C — The story (immediately below the reveal)**

This is where the buyer scrolls and reads. The intake content does the work, but the *order* matters: identity facts first (village, family, what they love), then specificity that activates senses (favorite meal, daily walk to school, teacher's name), then the kid's own voice (quote in their words). The reciprocity lever is being charged through this entire block — we're giving the buyer everything we know before asking for anything.

This block doesn't need new copy from me. The existing intake fields render it. But the *display order* on the page should be: name → photo → one-line → village → loves → school day → teacher quote → kid quote. Not a wall of fields. A story shape.

**Moment D — The ask (this is the conversion moment)**

The first draft gave this four lines. Here's the rewrite, applying levers 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7.

Page structure for this block:

```
[Step indicator, top of block]
Step 1: Bought your shirt ✓     Step 2: Met [FirstName] ✓     Step 3: Stay in their life

[Headline, large]
You know him now. Stay in his life.

[Body, three short paragraphs]
The shirt is how you met [FirstName]. $25 a month is how you stay.

Here's what $25 actually does: $11 for school fees, $9 for posho and
beans every day at lunch, $5 for the campus clinic the day malaria hits.
That's it. Not "support" or "impact" — that's the line items.

If you stay, you'll see what happens next. Did he pass his exams. What
he wrote in his Friday letter. Whether his English keeps getting better.
The shirt was how you met him. The rest of his life is on the other
side of $25 a month.

[Primary CTA, large gold button]
Stay with [FirstName] — $25/month

[Below the button, smaller, in mid-gray]
[FirstName]'s sponsorship spot is held for you for 7 days.
After that, the next person who lands on this page can claim it.

[Secondary off-ramp, smaller still]
Not ready? We'll send you a note from him in a week either way.
```

What's loaded into this block, lever by lever:

- **Lever 1 (endowed progress):** the three-step indicator at the top. The buyer has already completed Steps 1 and 2 — the page treats Step 3 as completion of an in-progress journey, not the start of a new commitment.
- **Lever 2 (identity > price):** the headline is "You know him now. Stay in his life." — pure identity. Price never appears in the headline. The button reads *Stay with [FirstName]* — also identity. The dollar amount is implementation detail under the button, not the lead.
- **Lever 3 (visceral breakdown):** the $11/$9/$5 line items. Specific. Sensory ("posho and beans every day at lunch"). Calculated, not arbitrary. And the explicit voice flag — *"Not 'support' or 'impact' — that's the line items"* — reinforces the brand voice on the page itself, which a sharp buyer will register.
- **Lever 5 (real scarcity):** *"[FirstName]'s sponsorship spot is held for you for 7 days. After that, the next person who lands on this page can claim it."* This is the strongest single sentence in the whole block, IF the mechanic is real. Engineering must actually hold the spot and actually release it. If we ship the copy without the mechanic, we violate the no-fake-scarcity rule and damage trust.
- **Lever 6 (reciprocity):** the entire Moment C above (the kid's full story) charges reciprocity. The headline *"You know him now"* names the gift we just gave. The ask follows.
- **Lever 7 (the second mystery):** the third paragraph — *"If you stay, you'll see what happens next. Did he pass his exams. What he wrote in his Friday letter. Whether his English keeps getting better."* The first mystery (who's my kid) just resolved. This paragraph opens the second mystery (what happens to him), and frames sponsorship as the way the buyer stays in it.
- **Lever 4 (pre-commitment) is not on this surface** — it goes on Surface 12 (checkout) so the buyer arrives here already pre-committed to the unboxing moment.

The off-ramp is intentional: *"Not ready? We'll send you a note from him in a week either way."* This does three things. It removes pressure (which protects against reactance), it leaves the buyer in our email orbit, and it sets up a second open loop — they're going to wonder what the note says, which gives us a second conversion shot a week later.

**Moment E — Post-yes (the moment they click the button)**

This is where the first-draft doc ended. But the moment immediately *after* the buyer says yes is where identity locks in or doesn't. This is its own surface — see Surface 13 below.

### Surface 11 — Day-of-reveal SMS (new, optional)

We don't currently send this. I'd recommend adding it — it's the single highest-leverage notification BAN could send. Trigger: USPS delivery confirmation webhook fires "delivered." Buyer gets a text. We don't have the buyer's number, so we can't link to /[N] directly — we link to beanumber.org and let them surface the number from the shirt.

| Angle | Copy |
| --- | --- |
| Anticipation | **Your shirt's at the door. Open it, find the number, then go to beanumber.org. Your kid is on the other side of that.** |
| Inversion | The shirt landed. We still don't know what number is on it. You're about to know first. |
| Promise | A real kid is on your porch right now. Open the package and go meet them at beanumber.org. |

**Pick: Anticipation.** Explicit action prompt at exactly the right second. This is the surface that converts the unboxing into a *shared social moment* — they're more likely to film themselves opening the box if we prompted them right when it arrived.

Engineering note: USPS API exposes delivery webhooks; would need to be wired to phone numbers we collect at checkout (we currently only collect email — adding phone as optional at checkout would unlock this). Crucially, this SMS does *not* require us to know which number went to which buyer — the buyer surfaces it themselves. So the only new infrastructure is (a) collect phone at checkout, (b) wire USPS delivery webhook to send the templated SMS. No per-buyer lookup needed.

### Surface 12 — Checkout pre-commitment checkbox (new, optional)

A small, optional, freely-chosen public commitment at checkout. Cialdini's commitment-and-consistency research says these stick. Practically: the buyer who checks this box is dramatically more likely to actually film the unboxing, which then feeds back as UGC and as a more memorable peak moment for them.

Placement: Stripe Checkout's custom fields or a pre-checkout interstitial. Below the cart summary, above the payment fields. Unchecked by default — has to be freely chosen to count, per Cialdini.

| Angle | Copy |
| --- | --- |
| Anticipation | **[ ] I'll film the moment I open the box and send it to BAN.** |
| Inversion | **[ ] I want to be one of the people who shows up at the door for their kid.** |
| Promise | **[ ] Count me in for the unboxing — I'll send you the photo or clip when it lands.** |

**Pick: Anticipation.** Direct and concrete. "Film" is more specific than "share" — the buyer pictures the act, which makes the commitment more behavioral and less abstract. The downstream confirmation email (Surface 7) should mention back the commitment they made, reinforcing consistency.

Engineering note: Stripe Checkout's `custom_fields` API supports a `dropdown` type that can be coerced to a yes/no. Or a pre-checkout splash page handles the same thing without Stripe field constraints. The buyer's selection has to be stored on the donor record so we can reference it later in drip copy.

### Surface 13 — Post-yes / first-month identity install (new)

The moment immediately after a buyer clicks "Stay with [FirstName] — $25/month" is where identity locks in or doesn't. The subscription-retention research is clear: the first 30 days are when status-quo bias either crystallizes around "I'm Aaron's sponsor" or doesn't. Almost everything that affects 12-month retention is set in this window.

This surface is two pieces: the on-page confirmation immediately after the click, and a triggered email an hour later.

**The on-page confirmation (replaces the Stripe-default thank-you page):**

| Angle | Copy |
| --- | --- |
| Anticipation | **You're in [FirstName]'s life now. He doesn't know yet. He will.** |
| Inversion | **You're his sponsor. There's no one else. Just you.** |
| Promise | **You're in. Here's what happens next: a letter from his teacher in 30 days, a photo from him before the end of the term, and his face every time you check your phone.** |

**Pick: Anticipation.** "He doesn't know yet. He will." installs the identity (you are now a person in someone's life) and opens a third mystery (when will he know, what will he say). Inversion is the close second; Promise is too information-dense for the post-click moment.

Below that line, a short bulleted list of what's coming (open-loop heavy):
- *Sometime in the next two weeks: his teacher Tonny writes you a letter. The first time he does, the kids watch him write it.*
- *Within 30 days: a photo from the campus, with [FirstName] in it somewhere.*
- *Each term: report cards, including the subjects he's worst at — we don't hide those.*
- *Anytime: you can come visit. Kevin will help you make it happen.*

That bulleted block is doing two jobs: setting expectations (so the buyer doesn't churn when the inbox is quiet for 10 days) and seeding multiple open loops that the buyer's brain will track. Zeigarnik tension keeps them subscribed across the early weeks.

**The triggered email (1 hour later):**

Subject: *You're [FirstName]'s sponsor. Here's what we've already told him.*

Body sketch:

> Hey [FirstName-of-sponsor],
>
> Quick note. The minute you clicked the button, we sent a message to Simon on the campus. Tomorrow morning over there (it's already tomorrow there now, actually — they're 8 hours ahead) Simon is going to walk into [Kid's first name]'s classroom and tell him he has a sponsor. He doesn't know your name yet. He's going to ask.
>
> Tell us what you want him to know. One sentence. Two. Whatever you want. We'll pass it on. Either reply to this email or use this link: [/sponsor/[code]/intro]
>
> — Kevin

This email's job is to convert the abstract subscription into a *specific human relationship*. The sponsor is given an explicit small action — write one sentence — that further activates commitment-consistency. By the time they finish that sentence, they're psychologically locked in for the next year, because they've now actively introduced themselves to a specific named child.

Engineering note: this triggers off the `customer.subscription.created` Stripe webhook. The /sponsor/[code]/intro endpoint stores the intro message on the sponsorship record and surfaces it to Simon's intake queue for translation/delivery. We have the infrastructure for this already — it's a new template and a new route.

## 5. Ship order

Section 0 is the launch. Everything below is "if and only if data demands it."

**Launch (one commit, four pieces):**

1. **Hero on the homepage.** *One shirt. One kid. Open the bag to meet them.*
2. **The brand tagline, deployed everywhere it fits one line.** *The shirt is how you meet them. $25 a month is how you stay.*
3. **Reveal-page CTA on /[N].** Photo, name, three sentences, button. Per Section 0.
4. **One drip email.** Subject *Your kid is waiting.* Body per Section 0.

Single dependency before launch: rule 2 of the framework. The reveal page only lands if the kid's intake is rich (name, face, village, what they love, teacher, quote). Audit the canonical roster's intake quality before shipping the reveal-page rewrite. If intake is sparse on a meaningful share of kids, fix intake first.

**Post-launch additions, in priority order — only if conversion data on the launch four says we need them:**

- **Add a drip day 3.** Section 4's drip-day-3 candidate (the "this is where your kid is, right now" email) is the obvious first add if the curiosity gap collapses between purchase and arrival. Don't ship preemptively.
- **Add a drip day 5–7.** Shipping notification. Useful if drip-day-3 alone doesn't sustain the wait.
- **Add the post-yes 1-hour intro email (Surface 13).** If month-2 retention is weaker than expected, this is the identity-install lever. Engineering is small — a triggered email and a /sponsor/[code]/intro route.
- **Add real 7-day spot-hold scarcity (Lever 5).** Engineering is non-trivial: actually holding the spot, actually releasing it. Don't ship the copy without the mechanic. Worth it if reveal-page conversion is below the 6% industry benchmark after a real sample.
- **Add the checkout pre-commitment checkbox (Surface 12).** UGC and unboxing follow-through lever. Add if the unboxing-share rate is lower than we want.
- **Add the day-of-reveal SMS (Surface 11).** Requires collecting phone at checkout + USPS delivery webhook wiring. Add when other surfaces are stable.

What never ships under this strategy: gated mystery on the existing direct-donation / direct-$25-give flows. Those flows serve buyers who don't want to wait. The mystery is the shirt-buyer path, not the only path.

## 6. Risks + how to read them

**Risk 1: Mystery reads as withholding.** If a buyer perceives the mystery as marketing manipulation rather than a real mechanic, trust collapses. Mitigation: the mechanic *is* real and our copy says so directly. There's no matching anywhere — shirts get printed with numbers, sit on a shelf by size and color, and whichever's next ships. Neither Kevin nor the BAN system knows what number went out until the buyer surfaces it by looking it up. "We don't know either" is the honest line, and it's the line we use. We never say "we have a special surprise" or "we picked the perfect kid for you" — both would be lies.

**Risk 2: The reveal disappoints.** If the kid page is thin when the buyer hits it, the entire wait was for nothing. Mitigation: rule 2 of the framework. Don't ship the mystery copy until we've audited intake quality across the canonical roster. Where intake is sparse, fix intake first. (Intentionally not citing a count — roster size drifts and "the canonical roster" is the safer reference.)

**Risk 3: We lose the cold buyer.** Some buyers want to see the kid before they commit. Mitigation: the /campus or partner-transparency pages still exist and still show the kids we've already revealed publicly. The mystery is on the *shirt-buying* path, not the only on-ramp. A donor who wants to give $25 without buying a shirt can still meet a kid first via the existing partner pages.

**Risk 4: Voice drift.** "Mystery" copy invites cinematic, mysterious-sounding language that violates voice rules. Mitigation: rule 3. If a candidate sounds like a movie trailer, it's wrong. The voice we want is closer to "calm confident insider" than "mystery box reveal." Short sentences. Specific places. Real names of teachers and meals.

**Risk 5: Sponsorship conversion math doesn't move.** It's possible the mystery angle lifts t-shirt sales but does nothing for monthly conversion on the /[N] page. Mitigation: ship Section 0's four pieces, measure for 60 days, then add levers from Section 3.5 in priority order — drip day 3 first, then post-yes identity install, then 7-day spot-hold scarcity, then pre-commitment checkbox, then SMS. Industry benchmark for one-time → monthly conversion is around 6%; Section 0's CTA copy (identity-led headline, school-day breakdown, loss-framed close) should land at or above that, with headroom to climb as levers are added. If conversion is below benchmark after a real sample, the first place to look is intake quality (rule 2) — sparse kid pages can't deliver a peak — and the second is whether the brand tagline actually appears above the CTA. Treat the funnel like a CRO problem with named levers, not a vibes problem.
