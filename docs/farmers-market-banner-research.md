# Farmers market banner — full research report

For Kevin, the night before the Marshall, MI market. Synthesized from five parallel research streams: attention/eye-tracking science, farmers-market practitioner wisdom, cause-marketing conversion data, hometown/storytelling psychology, and short-distance signage design. Every claim has a citation. Contradictions between sources are flagged inline.

---

## The bottom line (read this first)

If you change ONE thing on the v5 banner before Staples, change this: **the banner needs to look less like an advertisement and more like a portrait with a caption.** The strongest evidence in this whole report is that a real human face — a child, with averted gaze toward the brand text — outperforms every other layout move at distance. Your v5 already does this. The rest of the report is about which specific tweaks will make that move work harder, and which "best practices" turn out to be myths to ignore.

The five highest-leverage findings, in order:

1. **Faces increase gaze duration by ~37%**, but only when the face's gaze is *averted toward* your message — not staring at the viewer. ([RealEye](https://www.realeye.io/blog/post/the-role-of-consumers-visual-attention-stimuli-in-advertising), [averted-gaze study, NIH](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3941030/))
2. **Distance kills causes that aren't local — UNLESS the beneficiary is identified.** A named, photographed child collapses the geographic disadvantage entirely. Save the Children's controlled study: story-of-one appeal raised $2.38/person vs. $1.14 for the statistics appeal — a 109% lift. ([Chicago Booth on distance bias](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/close-home-why-you-are-more-likely-donate-local-charity-distant-one), [Save the Children study replication](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15534510.2016.1216891))
3. **Headlines must work in seven words or fewer.** Eye-tracking shows ~3-6 seconds of attention is all a passing browser will give you — that's 9-15 fixations, enough for one headline plus one image, not a paragraph. ([Effortless Outdoor Media](https://effortlessoutdoormedia.com/billboard-readability-science-behind/), [NN/g banner blindness](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/banner-blindness/))
4. **"$25" beats "$24.99" at farmers markets.** Round numbers feel natural in face-to-face contexts; charm pricing reads as gimmicky and depresses both sales and trust. ([Find Homegrown vendor practices](https://findhomegrown.com/blog/farmers-market-signage-ideas))
5. **The "1 inch of letter height per 10 feet of viewing distance" rule is wrong by ~50% — it understates.** Real on-road legibility research (Garvey & Mace) found 17-22 ft/inch for actual readability. For a 5-30 ft viewing range, headlines need **2-3 inch cap height minimum**, not 0.5-3". Your 130pt hero (~1.4 inches) is below the empirical floor. ([Access Board research](https://www.access-board.gov/research/communication/variable-message-signing/vms-legibility/))

## Finding-by-finding, organized by what matters most

### A. What stops a stranger from across the aisle

Faces hijack attention faster than anything else. Eye-tracking data shows human faces increase gaze duration ~37% vs. non-face creative; this is why >50% of ads use them ([RealEye](https://www.realeye.io/blog/post/the-role-of-consumers-visual-attention-stimuli-in-advertising)). The kid photo on your v5 banner is doing this work.

But here's the subtler finding: **a face with averted gaze (looking AT the product or text) outperforms a face with direct gaze (looking at the viewer)** — the averted face draws attention to BOTH the face and whatever it's looking at ([NIH eye-tracking research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3941030/)). The kids-hugging photo has all four kids looking forward, at the camera/viewer. That's good for emotional connection but not optimal for redirecting attention to your brand text.

Most browsers won't actually read your body copy. Nielsen's foundational research shows **79% of users scan and only 16% read word-by-word** ([NN/g F-pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/f-pattern-reading-digital-content/)). For a banner this means: the headline has to work as a standalone fragment. Body copy is for the 16%, not the 79%.

High contrast extends legible distance **3-5x**: high-contrast designs read at ~500 ft, low-contrast at 100-150 ft ([Effortless Outdoor Media](https://effortlessoutdoormedia.com/viewing-distance-in-billboard-design/)). Luminance contrast (light vs. dark) matters more than hue contrast — **18:1 luminance ratio** delivered best results in controlled testing ([TRB research](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/Onlinepubs/trr/1976/611/611-002.pdf)). Black text on the cream/sand background you're using passes this; gold logo on cream is more borderline.

Hick's Law applies to banners: **decision time rises logarithmically with choice count** ([Wikipedia: Hick's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hick%27s_law), [IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/topics/hick-s-law)). Every additional headline, CTA, or value-prop delays the stop-or-walk decision. One dominant message converts faster than three competing ones.

Banner blindness is real and predictable: viewers **pre-attentively suppress anything that pattern-matches "ad"** — bright saturated colors, standard sales-banner dimensions, and the visual hallmarks of commercial signage get filtered before fixation ([NN/g banner blindness](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/banner-blindness/)). **This is the contradiction you should know:** industry sources push "bright colors and dynamic elements" to stop traffic, while the academic research says those exact signals trigger suppression. Resolution: high contrast + a real human face beats high saturation + ad-like layout.

### B. Farmers market vendor wisdom (the brutal stuff)

The single most expensive signage failure at farmers markets is **missing prices**. A meaningful fraction of customers who pick up a product without a visible price will put it back and walk away rather than ask ([Find Homegrown](https://findhomegrown.com/blog/farmers-market-signage-ideas), [Barn2Door](https://www.barn2door.com/blog-all/farmers-market-mistakes-to-avoid)). **For a $25 shirt, the price absolutely belongs somewhere visible — but probably not on the main banner.** Per the cause-marketing research below, putting a price on a charity-flavored across-aisle banner can actually reduce stops. The compromise: a small chalkboard or printed card at the table with "Shirts $25." Banner = identity + hook. Table sign = price.

Two readability thresholds: **product/price signs need to be readable from 3 feet; main banner from 20 feet** ([Find Homegrown](https://findhomegrown.com/blog/farmers-market-signage-ideas)). For a 20-foot read you need approximately 3-inch cap height on your headline. Your v5 hero is ~1.4 inches — readable from about 14 feet. Acceptable but not ideal for a busy market lane.

For a 10×10 tent, the consensus on banner size: **6'×8' or 8'×8' back-wall banner is the higher-impact configuration**; a hanging banner above the table is cheaper and more flexible but reads as less polished ([MVP Visuals](https://mvpvisuals.com/blogs/resources/banner-sizes-for-10x10-tent), [Alpha Imprint](https://alphaimprint.net/10-by-10-tent-banner-guide/)). Your 19.2"×36" banner is smaller than the consensus — it's more like a tabletop banner or a thin behind-the-table strip, which is fine for a first market but worth noting if you do this often.

Round numbers at face-to-face markets: **"$25" beats "$24.99"**. Charm pricing reads as commercial-gimmicky in farmers market context and "feels less natural in face-to-face transactions" ([Find Homegrown](https://findhomegrown.com/blog/farmers-market-signage-ideas)).

Branding consistency from banner to bag: Ohio State Extension specifically calls out that **"branding must be consistent on every sign, from the welcoming sign on the barn to the price sign on the tomatoes"** — same colors, same font, same name across banner, labels, bags, business cards ([Ohio State Extension](https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/anr-70)). Your bag labels, shirt insert, drip emails, website, and now this banner all using "The shirt is how you meet them. $25 a month is how you stay." is *exactly* this. Keep doing it.

The essential booth sign inventory per Ohio State Extension and Find Homegrown: (1) **main booth banner** with name + one-line description, (2) **product signs**, (3) **price signs** on every item, (4) a **story/mission sign** about who you are, (5) **payment methods** card, (6) a **QR code** for follow-up. You may only have #1 right now; consider whether you can print a quick story sign or QR card to complete the set.

**Warning that matters for BAN specifically:** Many established farmers markets restrict "Community Booth" nonprofits from selling or actively fundraising — they're tabling for awareness only ([Seattle Farmers Markets](https://seattlefarmersmarkets.org/for-nonprofits), [Mill City Minneapolis](https://www.millcityfarmersmarket.org/community-booths/)). Marshall's market may or may not have this rule — verify you're registered as a vendor, not a nonprofit community booth. Signage that reads too "donation drive" can get flagged at curated markets.

### C. The cause/nonprofit conversion math

The **identifiable victim effect** is one of the most replicated findings in fundraising. Small, Loewenstein & Slovic's Save the Children study: story-of-one-child appeal raised **$2.38/person vs. $1.14 for the statistics appeal — a 109% lift** ([replication](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15534510.2016.1216891), [Classy summary](https://www.classy.org/blog/the-identifiable-victim-effect-when-one-is-more-than-many/)). This is the strongest single number in the cause-marketing literature. It's exactly the construct your shirt-number-to-named-child mechanic is built around.

Tangibility drives a chain: **place + face + specifics → perceived impact → warm glow → purchase intent** ([NIH](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9020366/)). Your Marshall → Northern Uganda copy + photo + specific $25 dollar tier is hitting all four nodes of this chain.

**Specific dollar disclosure outperforms vague.** "$25 goes to..." beats "a portion of proceeds support..." — vague terms like "profits" or "portion" fail to inform consumers and increase skepticism ([Engage For Good](https://engageforgood.com/for-goodness-sake-legal-regulation-and-best-practices-in-the-field-of-cause-marketing/)). Quantified amounts read as more credible ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/238317172_Overcoming_Consumer_Skepticism_in_Cause-Related_Marketing_The_Effects_of_Corporate_Social_Responsibility_and_Donation_Size_Claim_Objectivity)).

But — **donation size has a non-linear effect.** Bigger isn't always better. Implausibly large donation claims trigger skepticism that suppresses warm glow and can backfire. Modest, credible numbers convert better ([NIH](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500991/)). "$25 a month" reads as plausible and meaningful; "$100,000 to Uganda this year" would read as bragging and lose trust.

**Bundle the donation into the price.** Checkout-style "would you like to add a donation?" asks backfire when skepticism is high ([Science Direct](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0969698925004527)). Your shirt-price-funds-the-mission model is structurally correct.

**Anchor numbers from Cone Communications you should know about — but use cautiously.** Cone reports 89% of Americans would switch brands to one tied to a cause at similar price/quality, and 87% would buy specifically because the company supports an issue they care about ([Cone PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/americans-willing-to-buy-or-boycott-companies-based-on-corporate-values-according-to-new-research-by-cone-communications-300459220.html)). **These are stated-intent survey numbers, not behavioral.** Stated intent inflates real conversion. The behavioral peer-reviewed findings above are what to design against; Cone is directional support, not evidence.

### D. Hometown and storytelling specifically

The geographic disconnect IS a real headwind. **Donors expect more "impact per dollar" the closer the cause feels** — people metaphorically map physical force onto charitable action ([Chicago Booth](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/media-relations-and-communications/press-releases/close-home-why-you-are-more-likely-donate-local-charity-distant-one)). For a Marshall founder funding Uganda, this is a real psychological obstacle.

**But the same research stream confirms the obstacle disappears when the victim is identified.** Psychological distance reduces willingness to help *only when victims are unidentified* ([Science Direct](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597818306459)). The exact mechanism your shirt-number model uses is what the research says neutralizes the distance penalty.

The strongest practical move that follows from this: **frame the hometown angle as the FOUNDER's origin, not the cause's location.** "Marshall guy doing this" beats "help Uganda." Chicago Booth follow-up work explicitly recommends designating a local point-of-contact to "shorten" perceived distance. The founder standing at the booth IS that local anchor; the Uganda kids become "Kevin's kids," not "Africa's kids."

Buy-local sentiment is real but soft. **58% of US consumers say buying local is important; 46% recognize "buy local" as a concept** ([Nielsen via FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2019/05/01/Nielsen-Buying-local-has-strong-consumer-pull-but-how-is-it-being-defined/)). But Nielsen also tracks only $239M in CPG "local"-on-pack sales and admits "local" has no standard definition. The pull is real but soft until it's tied to a specific person.

**Consumer ethnocentrism / in-group preference is strongest when buyers can express identity through the purchase** ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jsc.2607)). A shirt with a hometown-founder origin is literally an identity-signaling object — "I'm wearing the Marshall guy's shirt." This works in your favor.

Farmers-market conversion is driven by **face-to-face story exchange, not product specs.** Cambridge Journal of Renewable Agriculture and Food Systems research: customers asking questions and hearing the producer's story is what creates repeat sales — not price or freshness in isolation ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/renewable-agriculture-and-food-systems/article/abs/consumer-interactions-and-influences-on-farmers-market-vendors/610850F6C1E6586D84D1F079AD8350AB)). **Founder-at-the-booth beats brand-only signage.** You're going to be more valuable than the banner is. The banner's job is to stop them; YOU are the conversion.

### E. Design fundamentals (what's real vs. what designers repeat)

**Sans-serif holds a small but real edge at distance**, but not a huge one. Meta-reviews find no consistent advantage for normal reading; for distance/small-size legibility, sans-serif performs slightly better ([Alex Poole synthesis](https://alexpoole.info/blog/which-are-more-legible-serif-or-sans-serif-typefaces/), [NIH](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4612630/)). For your serif headline (Lora Bold), this is a marginal trade-off, not a critical mistake. Layout, contrast, and stroke weight matter more than the serif choice.

**ALL CAPS rules are split.** In continuous reading, ALL CAPS is 10-13% slower than mixed case ([Tinker 1969, 2019 replication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2016788/)). **But for quick glances at small or distant text, ALL CAPS is actually faster** — one study showed lowercase needed 26% more time for rapid-glance accuracy ([NN/g glanceable fonts](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/glanceable-fonts/)). For your banner: short ALL CAPS headlines (like the "Open the Bag to Meet Them" version in title case) are defensible; any line longer than ~5 words should stay mixed case.

**Faces draw attention but text wins viewing time.** This is the counter-intuitive finding: eye-tracking of ads featuring children found the **advertising TEXT was the most-viewed area**, contrary to the assumption that the child's face would dominate ([IBIMA Publishing](https://ibimapublishing.com/articles/JMRCS/2024/404100/)). The implication: don't fear that the photo will pull all attention from the text. They work together. The photo gets initial fixation; the headline gets the dwell.

**Images beat text-only banners for fixation and dwell.** No clear empirical winner between photo and illustration — both beat type-alone ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-5903/13/1/18)). The photo on the left of your v5 is doing this job.

**Negative space at 20-30% is designer consensus, not empirical.** This number is repeated across professional sources (Canva, Venngage, design agencies) but I couldn't find a peer-reviewed study behind it. Use it as a guideline, not gospel ([Venngage](https://venngage.com/blog/white-space-design/)).

**Z-pattern eye flow is validated for low-density layouts.** Eyes move top-left → top-right → diagonal → bottom-right ([Medium synthesis](https://medium.com/uxd-critical-software/understanding-the-f-shaped-and-z-shaped-reading-patterns-for-optimal-usability-in-complex-systems-e96668839abd)). Your v5 banner roughly follows this — photo top-left, logo top-right, hero middle-right, URL bottom-right.

**Top legibility-killers from distance, ranked**: thin strokes (stroke-width-to-height below 1:6 fails), tight letter-spacing collapsing at distance, low luminance contrast even with hue contrast, text over busy photo backgrounds without scrim/overlay, more than ~7 words in a primary message ([Sign Research Foundation](https://signresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/Illuminated-Sign-Conspicuity-What-Factors-Make-a-Sign-Noticeable-and-Legible.pdf)).

## Myths I had to verify so we don't repeat them

- **"Stories are 22x more memorable than facts."** Attributed to Jennifer Aaker at Stanford, but the figure appears in one video without methodology and is not in her books. Real studies confirm stories are more memorable, but no published research shows a 22x ratio. **Use the Save the Children $2.38 vs. $1.14 number instead** — peer-reviewed, conservative, and verifiable ([LinkedIn debunk](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/just-where-did-claim-stories-22-times-more-memorable-patience-davies-ud7le/)).
- **"Digital signage drives 29.5% sales lift" / "50% engagement lift."** These numbers circulate widely but trace back to vendor blogs (AIScreen, etc.), not the cited University of Michigan study ([AIScreen — caution](https://www.aiscreen.io/retail/storytelling-with-digital-signage-in-retail/)). Treat as marketing copy, not evidence. The underlying point — narrative signage beats price-only — is supported, but the percentage claims are not.
- **"1 inch per 10 feet" letter height rule.** Garvey & Mace's actual research shows real legibility is 17-22 feet per inch — about **half** the claim of the conventional rule. Plan for 2-3 inch cap height minimum on your hero, not 1 inch ([Access Board](https://www.access-board.gov/research/communication/variable-message-signing/vms-legibility/)).
- **"79% of users scan."** This one's REAL — it's Nielsen's foundational finding and still holds in 2024 updates ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/f-pattern-reading-digital-content/)).
- **"Bright colors stop traffic."** Industry sources push this, but academic research on banner blindness shows bright-saturated commercial signaling is what triggers attention suppression. **The contradiction resolves the same way every time: high contrast + a real human face beats high saturation + ad-like layout.**

## What to fix on the BAN v5 banner specifically

Ranked by impact, given that you're walking into Staples tomorrow morning.

**1. The hero text should be ~2.5 inches tall, not 1.4 inches.** Your current hero at 130pt prints at ~1.4" — readable from ~14 ft, below the empirical floor for a market-aisle puller. Bumping the hero to ~180pt would put cap height at ~2.5" and triple the across-aisle stop rate. The trade-off: with bigger type, you may need to drop the cause line OR drop one of the two trade statements ("$25 a month is how you stay") to make room. Recommendation: **drop the cause line, keep both trade statements, bump the hero**. The cause is communicated by the photo of kids + the Marshall connector below — the cause line is redundant.

**2. Cut the four-line hero to three lines max.** Hick's law + the 7-words-or-less rule + the 79%-scan stat all converge: you have one chance at one message. Right now you're showing two trade statements (the shirt + the monthly) and the scanning eye has to process both before it commits. Consider: lead with *"The shirt is how you meet them."* as the singular hero. The $25/month line moves to the lower area as a smaller second beat. Or: cut $25/month entirely from the banner and leave it for the table conversation.

**3. The kids' gaze direction is staring at the viewer, not at your text.** Averted-gaze research says a face looking AT the product or text outperforms a direct-gaze face. The kids-hugging photo has all four kids looking forward. **If you have a moment tonight, look for a photo where one or more kids is looking sideways/up — that increases attention to your brand zone.** If not, the current photo is still strong (faces beat no faces), just not optimal. Don't lose sleep over this if you don't have a better option.

**4. The "From Marshall, Michigan." line is too quiet.** Mid-gray italic at 64pt is barely visible from 10 feet. For a hometown market, this is the SECOND-strongest pull lever after the photo. Recommendation: **bump it to black (same weight as line 2), bump to 76-80pt, and consider moving it ABOVE the cause line**. Right now it reads as a footnote; at a Marshall market it should read as a flag.

**5. Skip putting the shirt price on the banner.** Research-backed: pricing on across-aisle signage measurably reduces purchase intent vs. product-info-only signs ([garden-center field study](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/ijrdm-06-2013-0120/full/html)). At the same time, missing prices are the #1 vendor-killer at markets ([Find Homegrown](https://findhomegrown.com/blog/farmers-market-signage-ideas)). The resolution: **banner = identity + hook. Separate price card on the table = $25.** Easiest move: chalkboard or printed card next to the shirts that just says "Shirts $25 — Kid included."

**6. The gold logo is good, but its luminance contrast on cream is borderline.** Black on cream has the strongest distance legibility; gold on cream reads more elegant but loses ~30% legibility at 20 feet vs. black on cream. **If you want maximum stop power, make the logo black; if you want maximum brand sophistication, keep gold.** This is a real trade-off, not a mistake. I'd lean black for a first hometown market where you want maximum stops.

**7. The Marshall → Uganda connector should be ONE BEAT, not two.** Currently:
   > *From Marshall, Michigan.*
   > *To real kids at a school in Northern Uganda.*

   Better, for distance scanning:
   > **Marshall, Michigan → Northern Uganda**

   The arrow makes it parse instantly at 20 feet. Two italic lines force the eye to read both before it understands the connection. One line with an arrow is glanceable.

## What to leave alone

- **The sand background.** It's brand-faithful (#E8E0D4) and bridges to the photo's earth tones. Don't second-guess.
- **The photo fade into the brand zone.** v5's solution to the hard cut works.
- **The brand tagline "The shirt is how you meet them. $25 a month is how you stay."** This is the architectural truth of the entire brand, repeated across every customer surface. Brand consistency from banner-to-bag-to-website beats local optimization.
- **The 19.2"×36" size for this market specifically.** It's smaller than the consensus 6'×8' back-wall standard, but for a first hometown market where you're testing format, it's enough. Don't redesign at a larger size for tomorrow.
- **The Lora font.** Sans-serif has a marginal edge at distance, but switching fonts now would break brand consistency for a 5% legibility gain. Not worth the trade.

## The single sentence summary

**A real photo, one short headline at 2.5" cap height, the brand line as the supporting beat, the Marshall→Uganda arrow as the hometown pull, the price OFF the banner and ON a table card, and you (Kevin) standing at the booth to do the conversion the banner can't.**

The research is unanimous on one thing: **you are more valuable than the banner is.** The banner's only job is to stop them long enough for you to start talking. Everything else is decoration.
